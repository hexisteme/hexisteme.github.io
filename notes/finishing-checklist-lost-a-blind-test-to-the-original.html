I Applied an 18-Item Finishing Checklist to One Shot. My Blind A/B Test Picked the Original.

← hexisteme · notes · 2026-11-23

I turned finishing instructions from a public collection of code-rendered video prompts into an 18-item checklist and applied the 8 items that could change one 3.3-second Blender shot. In my blind A/B with identical re-encoding and a random assignment opened after the verdict, I picked the original as more finished, so the checklist was rejected without a revised second round.

On November 14 I watched two silent, re-encoded clips of the same 3.3-second shot, labeled A and B, and answered two questions: which one looks more finished, and can I use the one I picked. I picked A and said I would use it. When the assignment was opened, A was the original. B was a rework built from an 18-item finishing checklist, and the checklist was rejected that afternoon.

This note covers where the checklist came from, what could and could not be applied to the shot, how the blind comparison was set up, and what the rejection does and does not tell me.

The complaint behind it

My code-rendered explainer shorts had drawn a note from me: not enough detail, not enough finish. I wanted to test one specific remedy. A public collection of prompts for code-rendered videos contains a lot of explicit finishing instructions, things like spring easing, film grain and type hierarchy. If I distilled those into a checklist and applied it to one existing shot, would I see the difference with my eyes covered?

My main Claude session did the preparation and the rework. I was the only judge; no external model was called for the verdict.

From 703 prompts to 18 items

The collection had 703 prompts (motion 369, 3d 117, explainer 116, interactive 101). The session kept the explainer and motion prompts that were at least 600 characters long and were not partial prompts, which left 56. Across those 56, it counted how often each family of instruction appeared:

  • scene and time structure 79 %
  • easing and timing 64 %
  • resolution and fps 57 %
  • sound 57 %
  • text 55 %
  • camera 54 %
  • color 46 %
  • light and shadow 45 %
  • depth of field and blur 43 %
  • texture and grain 32 %
  • micro detail 20 %

The checklist had to satisfy a few rules, and a script checked them before the spec was locked:

  • between 12 and 20 instructions, written in my own words
  • each item backed by at least 3 prompts from the 56 that actually contain that instruction, checked by keyword
  • an inspection method per item, phrased for my pipeline (Blender keyframe renders)
  • no run of 8 consecutive words shared with any prompt

The prompt text itself was not copied into the checklist and was kept out of version control. The result had 18 items, from spring easing and staggered entrances to soft shadows, type hierarchy and the main subject filling the frame.

The shot, and what was frozen

The target was a 3.3-second shot rendered in Blender with a top orthographic camera. It was picked because it is pure code render, so the engine could stay fixed while only the finish changed, and because its frames showed what the complaint was about: an empty floor across the lower half of the frame, flat material and small text.

The spec froze a lot on purpose:

  • The engine stayed Blender. Switching to HTML would mix engine and finish, and the comparison would mean nothing.
  • Fact-bearing geometry stayed: object positions, trajectories and the camera projection. The orthographic top view is there so that angles on the table read true.
  • Pipeline code was out of scope. Changes could only go through the scene JSON (look settings and added objects). Anything the pipeline could not express was to be recorded as "pipeline unsupported."
  • No piling on effects to make the shot look busier.
  • The published video and its assets were not touched.

Acceptance criteria were measurable where possible. The rework had to match the original at 1080×1920, 30 fps and the same frame count. The caption band (y 1150 to 1440) had to stay readable for white captions: at the 0.5, 1.6 and 3.0 second frames, mean brightness at most 0.35 (original 0.218) and brightness standard deviation at most max(original × 2, 0.04) (original 0.027). The check scripts were written before the lock and pinned with sha256 so they could not be loosened later. Another rule: if I picked the original, a revised checklist and a second comparison could not count as the verdict.

What actually got applied: 8 of 18

The applied ledger split the 18 items into 8 applied and 10 not applied. In the scene file, the rework changed the keys of the four label objects, the sizes of three of them, and one look setting, light_scale. The other 35 original objects stayed as they were, and one object was added: a field of droplets called mist.

The 8 applied items:

  • Spring arrival: the 4 labels now arrive at scale 1.06 with power2.out, then settle back to 1.0 with sine.inOut 0.08 s later. Overshoot 6 %, under the 10 % limit.
  • No dead time: the droplets drift by 0.01 to 0.025 units over the 3.3 s. In the last 0.3 s window, the maximum frame difference rose from 0.003 to 0.021. The 2.72 s and 0.4 s silences in the shot card were intended, so they stayed; only the picture was kept alive.
  • Type hierarchy: the name labels got bigger (0.070 to 0.092, 0.076 to 0.088, 0.095 to 0.112), while the definition label stayed at 0.066. The renderer has a single font, Montserrat ExtraBold, so the weight and letter-spacing part of the instruction was pipeline unsupported.
  • Soft shadows: light_scale went from 1.0 to 1.3, which took the area light size from 1.2 to 1.56 while keeping center brightness. The empty floor's mean brightness moved from 0.224 to 0.226, which kept an earlier decision of mine: dark background, high contrast on the subject.
  • One still per beat before the full render: the session reviewed contact sheets over 4 rounds before rendering. Round 2 (sun 0.6, light_scale 1.6) brightened the floor and cut the subject's contrast, so it was rejected. Round 1 had shrunk the definition label, which broke the readability item, so that was reverted.
  • Looking real: the shot card describes the floor as a dark reenactment surface, so the session scattered 900 condensation droplets at radius 0.0014. They are scattered at random with no lines, to make them less likely to be misread as trajectories.
  • Surface texture: the same droplets break up the flat floor. High-frequency deviation on the empty floor went from 0.0019 to 0.0156 on the 1.6 s still (measured on a version with droplet radius 0.0019), and caption band deviation went from 0.0272 to 0.0228.
  • Readability on a phone: covered by the name-label enlargement above. The definition label was not shrunk.

The 10 not applied:

  • Already satisfied, 6 items. Motion blur: the pipeline's default shutter is 0.5 (180°), present in the original. Camera: locked top orthographic, owned by the shot card. Stagger: labels already enter at 0.25 s and 0.30 s. Color: the only saturated colors are amber and coral on the two main objects, and the heat effect lives inside an imagination balloon. Decoration: the only glow is a flash inside that balloon. Transitions: continuous inside the shot, match cut to the next.
  • Pipeline unsupported, 1 item: film grain. There is no compositing stage after render, and pipeline code was out of scope. The droplets give static texture, not new grain every frame.
  • Conflicts with an earlier decision of mine, 1 item: the background light gradient. It was tried. The renderer's key light position is fixed, so the direction of the gradient could not change, and lowering the sun brightened the whole floor. That conflicts with what I decided on October 29: keep the background dark and the subject high-contrast.
  • Locked by facts and continuity, 1 item: the subject filling the frame. Camera span and target are bound to a match cut with the next shot, which uses the same camera, and the sphere radii are bound to a ratio the content depends on.
  • Not applicable, 1 item: sound. Shots render silent; sound effects are placed later on the timeline.

That last locked item matters most for the conclusion, and I come back to it below.

The blind comparison

A script re-encoded both clips with identical settings (libx264 slow, crf 18, yuv420p, no audio, metadata stripped), then assigned A and B from an os.urandom seed. Both were 1080×1920 with 99 frames. I received a side-by-side file (A left, B right) plus A and B on their own, with the two questions. The assignment was opened only after my answer.

My answers were A for more finished, and yes, usable. On disclosure, A was the original and B the rework (seed 2058045285). The assignment was checked against the source files by mean absolute frame difference at 1.6 s:

  • A vs original 0.108, A vs rework 2.080
  • B vs original 2.084, B vs rework 0.131

So with the labels hidden, I chose the original as the more finished one. The rework, with its droplet texture, larger name labels, overshoot on arrival and softer shadows, did not read to me as better finish.

Rejected, without a second round

The verdict was rejection: the checklist does not go into the production checklist for these scenes. Under the spec's own rule, the checklist was not revised and re-compared. A second round run after seeing the loss would be tuned to beat me, and it could only be a side note.

Why the original won is an open question. I was not asked for reasons during the judging, so what follows is a guess written down as a guess: the droplets may have read as dust or stars rather than texture, or enlarging the name labels may have broken the balance the original had. Another inference from the ledger: my pipeline already had many of the collection's finishing instructions built in (motion blur, locked camera, stagger, restrained color, no decoration, continuous cuts), so the instructions left to add were not an improvement here.

The one large lever the collection had that my shots lack was the subject filling the frame. In this shot it was locked at the shot design stage: camera span, the match cut and the radii. A finishing pass could not reach it. That supports an observation, not a rule: the "not enough detail" complaint, at least for this shot, points at shot design (an empty lower half of the frame), not at a finishing pass.

What this does not show

This is one shot, one judge and one comparison. It does not show that finishing checklists hurt, or that the collection's instructions are wrong for other pipelines. It shows that on this shot, with this pipeline's defaults, the items that could be applied did not make me see more finish with my eyes covered.

The rework also changed one shot only, so its droplets and label sizes would not match the shots on either side. Adopting it would have meant applying it across the whole run of shots that share that look.

The checklist stays as reference material. The rejection has a reopening condition: if, in another episode, versions that put the subject-fills-the-frame item and the background light gradient into shot design beat the original in my blind judging in 2 out of 2 episodes, I reopen it. The next thing to test is that, at the shot design stage, not as a finishing pass.

FAQ

How many of the 18 checklist items could actually be applied to the shot?

8. The other 10 were already satisfied by the pipeline (6), unsupported by the pipeline (1, film grain), in conflict with an earlier decision to keep the background dark (1), locked by facts and continuity (1, the subject filling the frame), or not applicable because shots render silent (1).

How was the blind comparison kept blind?

Both clips were re-encoded with the same settings (libx264 slow, crf 18, yuv420p, no audio, metadata stripped), both were 1080x1920 with 99 frames, A and B were assigned from an os.urandom seed, and the assignment was opened only after the verdict. It was then checked by mean absolute frame difference at 1.6 s: A vs original 0.108, B vs rework 0.131.

Why didn't you fix the checklist and run the comparison again?

The spec said in advance that if the original was picked, a revised checklist and a second comparison could not count as the verdict. A round run after seeing the loss would be tuned to the judge.

Why did the original win?

I was not asked for reasons during judging, so the reasons are guesses: the droplets may have read as dust or stars rather than texture, or the larger name labels may have broken the original's balance. The pipeline also already had many of the instructions built in, such as motion blur, a locked camera, stagger and restrained color.

What would reopen the checklist?

If, in another episode, versions that put the subject-fills-the-frame item and the background light gradient into shot design beat the original in my blind judging in 2 out of 2 episodes.
