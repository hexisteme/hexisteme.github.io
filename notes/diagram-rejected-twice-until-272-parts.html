My YouTube Shorts Diagram Was Sent Back Twice Before a 272-Part Version Was Accepted

← hexisteme · notes · 2026-11-23

An open-source diagram skill drew one line of my explainer short at its Figure grade (48 solids); I gave it a conditional yes, then failed a second version built against six new checks for fill, contrast, shape, motion, text and facts. None of those checks measured detail; the version I accepted was built against the skill's Hero grade and had 272 registered parts.

On 2026-11-14 I ran an isolated trial of an open-source diagram skill called anatomy on one line of a short explainer video. The first version came back in under ten minutes with its own checks clean. I watched it and said: "If it's refined a bit more precisely, I'll use it." The second version was built against six new checks I wrote after that verdict, and I said: "It needs to be refined much more, in finer detail." That one was a fail. The first version was 48 solids; the third was counted differently, as 272 entries in a parts registry, and my verdict on it was: "I'll use it in this scene."

This note is about what my checks measured in each round, and the one thing none of them measured until the third round: whether the drawing was detailed enough.

The trial

anatomy is an MIT-licensed skill that builds isometric exploded diagrams as a single local HTML file. I installed it from an audited, pinned commit, scoped to two of my shorts projects and not globally, because a global skill adds catalog tokens to every session. Its README says it is slow on purpose, so cost was part of the acceptance criteria from the start.

The line I picked was the only one in its script that explains how something works: "By 1942, they'd pumped the water around it dry." It is about the drained polder around the former island of Schokland. Water inside a dike is pumped over the dike, only the inner level drops, and the old island stands up out of the dry bed. The script already cited the dates: pumping started on 7 January 1941, and on 9 September 1942 an average water level of 4.40 m below NAP was reached. The diagram maps onto a physical model: a glass tank holding lake, dike and polder, a pumping station on the dike, the island on the floor, and a gauge staff.

The episode had already been uploaded and scheduled, so the clip was a test and never went into the published video. The target was a 3 to 5 second vertical clip at 1080×1920, rendered through HyperFrames, since my pipeline already renders HTML that way.

Round 1: Figure grade, 48 solids

The skill's workflow starts by choosing a size grade. My first spec did not name one, and the first build used the Figure grade: 48 solids and 427 SVG paths. The skill's own verification passed. The line-end check reported line ends: 0 of 188 in both dark and light themes, the console was clean, and captures at desktop, phone and 4× zoom were taken. The geometry audit did not apply, because the drawing used no lathe or tube parts, which are the cases where the skill asks for it.

The cost was 9.7 minutes of wall clock and 227,847 tokens as reported by the subagent runtime. The final render took 14.9 s.

My verdict was the conditional one above. My main Claude session then diagnosed six problems: the diagram used only 45 % of the vertical frame, contrast was low, the island read as a ship, the pump showed no flow, the text was small, and the on-screen numbers had not been verified.

Round 2: six new checks, still Figure grade

Each diagnosis became an acceptance criterion:

  • Frame fill: diagram rows at least 55 % of the frame height, and no diagram rows at y ≥ 1250, where captions and the bottom UI sit. The check script was written by my main session and the spec forbade editing it or padding the height with card borders or labels.
  • Contrast: zero errors and zero warnings from the HyperFrames contrast check.
  • Shape: a frame shown to Codex with no name and no context must read as an island or mound with a village and church, and not as a ship.
  • Motion: pump rotation and flow in the discharge pipe visible, the outer lake level held while only the inner level drops, with easing on the drop.
  • Text: every glyph on screen at a font size of at least 36 px at 1080 width.
  • Facts: every date and number left on screen backed by two independent sources, or removed.

The blind read is the one I have full records for. Before showing anything to Codex, the subagent looked at its own first island shape, a long pointed white mound, decided it looked like a ship, and redrew it with an irregular outline, sand and grass layers, two dwelling mounds, gabled buildings, a church spire and trees. The question to Codex was: "Describe what you see in this picture and what the raised object in the middle is." It answered that the middle object looked like "a terrain model representing an island or high land", with white buildings like houses and a church on top. The words "Pumping from 7 Jan 1941" were on screen, so the pumping context came from the text, but neither the question nor the frame contained the place name or the word island.

Round 2 took 15 minutes 45 seconds and 177,938 tokens. Codex used 18,742 tokens on its own Plus quota.

Then came the second verdict, the fail. None of the criteria I had added asked whether the drawing was detailed enough. They checked fill, contrast, shape reading, motion, text size and facts. One number from my records shows the gap: the skill's line-end check on the round 2 version reported 117/168. That check was not among my round 2 criteria. I did not record why the count rose from round 1.

Round 3: Hero grade as acceptance criteria

The revision I approved after the fail raised the grade to Hero, using the skill's own grade description: one dense object, 80 to 150 parts, 2 to 4 hours, and the whole drawing inspected at 4×. The kind count, a required passing audit and the other thresholds below were criteria I added for this trial; the skill itself asks for its audit only when a drawing has tube, cable or round parts. My main session told me before I approved that it expected 1 to 2 hours and more than half a million tokens. The new criteria:

  • At least 120 parts and at least 25 distinct kinds, with repeated trees, piles or windows counted as one kind, and every part labeled with the parent part it sits on. Cloning parts to reach the count was forbidden.
  • The skill's geometry audit must pass, with overlaps fixed in geometry and not by turning the check off.
  • The island outline traced from a real map with a length-to-width ratio within ±15 % of the real one, the church, the harbour head with its lighthouse, and the pile wall around the island edge each drawn from two independent photo or plan sources, and the dike drawn as a sloped section.
  • 4× crops of six zones (pump, pipe joints, church, harbour and lighthouse, gauge, dike) with no gaps or overlaps, and zero floating line ends.
  • At least four secondary motions: impeller rotation, spray at the outfall, ripples on the water, and a wet band on the exposed bed turning to a dry color.

The parts count came from the build itself. Every drawing call registered one entry through a part(name, kind, parent) function, and a --parts flag dumped that registry, so the number was not counted by hand. One name is one solid or one pipe, and a pipe counts once however many segments it has, which is why this number is not directly comparable to the 48 solids of round 1. The result was 272 parts and 105 kinds, 68 of them kinds with a single part. 90 of the parts are piles, placed at real spacing from an 1816 specification and a heritage register entry; excluding them leaves 182. The record keeps both numbers so the piles are not read as padding. Parts I had planned but could not source, such as a berm on the dike, a pipe clamp and two village houses, were dropped. Sourced ones, such as an iceboat shed, a chestnut tree and a fog-horn house, were added. The six trees stayed as generic shapes, marked as having no position source.

The blind read took three tries with the identical question. The first answer called the raised mound, ringed by its pile wall, "a work space or vertical shaft surrounded by piles". The fix was to draw the mound, pile wall and harbour heads at the building scale of 1.2 units per metre instead of 10, which removed a tower-like mound. The second answer said: "The long raised object in the middle looks like a ship's hull." The fix was to replace the vertical island sides with three contour steps of soil and put six trees on top. The third answer read "land drawn as a small island or dike", with grass, trees and two houses, and added that it "could look like a ship because of the long outline, but the depiction is closer to terrain." The church was read only as a house. Both wrong readings were fixed in the drawing; the question never changed.

Before the verdict I was told the known remaining defects: the island's height exaggerated by the stepped sides, the island identified only on the third blind read with the church not recognized, a green rectangle on the island, and one chunk of pile wall lying on the dry bed. I judged with that list in hand and accepted the clip for the scene.

Round 3 took 1 hour 9 minutes 11 seconds of wall clock and 237,688 tokens, with 187 tool calls up to the end of sampling. The three Codex reads used 20,431, 35,626 and 28,254 tokens. Across all three rounds the record is about 95 minutes and 643,473 tokens. The per-round token figures and that total are subagent runtime reports; they leave out the main session and the Codex reads, which ran on separate quota.

A side measurement: the headless browser

The spec also required that the skill make no connections outside loopback while it ran. Reading the skill's source had found no external calls, and the diagram page itself loads nothing remote. Sampling with lsof every 2 s told a different story in round 1: 237 PID-and-connection pairs to 53 unique remote endpoints, almost all from the headless Chrome that the skill's verification scripts launch with a fresh profile and no background-networking flags. HyperFrames also fetched a monospace font from Google Fonts and, during its check, reached an AWS address I did not identify; I logged that separately and excluded it. Adding background-networking and component-update flags to the skill's Chrome launcher still left connections to six destinations in a 12 s probe, including what look like DNS-over-HTTPS probes to 8.8.8.8 and 8.8.4.4. Adding --host-resolver-rules=MAP * ~NOTFOUND , EXCLUDE localhost for file targets brought two 15 s probes to 0, and the round 3 sampling recorded 0 for the skill. A 2 s sample can miss short connections, so 0 here means none were observed.

What I would change

The grade was the lever, and in my first spec it was an unnamed default. Every criterion I wrote after the first verdict measured a property I could name from that verdict: fill, contrast, legibility, motion, facts. "More precise" did not name a property, so I translated it into six measurable ones, and the second version, built against them, got another no. The skill already had a written grade for detail, as a part range and 4× inspection, and the version I accepted is the first one built against it, with a kind count and a required audit I added on top. Next time, the grade goes into the spec before the first build.

Two things stay open. The church still reads as a house to a blind reader, so the shape check passed only conditionally. And this is one scene with one reviewer, me. I have not checked whether Hero grade from the start would cost less than the 95 minutes these three rounds took, or whether a Figure-grade diagram would be accepted for a simpler line.

FAQ

What changed between the first two versions and the accepted one?

The first two versions were built at the skill's Figure grade; the first had 48 solids and got a conditional yes, and the second was a fail. The third used the skill's Hero grade plus criteria I added for the trial: at least 120 parts and 25 distinct kinds with repeats counted as one kind, a parent for every part, a passing geometry audit, 4× crops of six zones, sourced landmarks and at least four secondary motions. It came out at 272 registered parts and 105 kinds, where a pipe counts as one part however many segments it has, so 272 and 48 are not counted the same way.

Were the 272 parts padded with cloned objects?

Cloning to reach the count was forbidden. 90 of the parts are piles placed at real spacing from an 1816 specification and a heritage register entry, and excluding them leaves 182. The count came from a registry every drawing call wrote to through part(name, kind, parent), dumped with a --parts flag, not from a hand count.

How was the blind shape check run?

A frame was shown to Codex with the same question each time and no place name or hint: describe what you see and what the raised object in the middle is. In the third round the first answer read a work space or shaft, the second read a ship's hull, and the third read a small island with trees and houses. Both wrong readings were fixed in the drawing, and the church was still read only as a house.

How much did the three rounds cost?

About 95 minutes and 643,473 tokens in total: 227,847, 177,938 and 237,688 tokens per round as reported by the subagent runtime, with the main session not included. The third round alone took 1 hour 9 minutes 11 seconds.

Did the diagram skill make network connections?

The skill's code and the diagram page made none, but the round 1 sampling found 237 PID-and-connection pairs to 53 endpoints, mostly from the headless Chrome its verification scripts launch, with HyperFrames connections also in that total: a logged Google Fonts download and, during its check, an AWS address whose purpose I did not confirm. Background-networking flags alone still left six destinations in a 12 s probe; adding --host-resolver-rules for file targets brought it to 0, and round 3 sampling recorded 0 for the skill.
